Four Eras, One Continuing Story

Philippine literature did not begin with a single book — it began with voices around a fire, continued through borrowed scripts and imposed tongues, and carries on today across screens and stages. This site walks through four eras of that story: pre-colonial, colonial, post-colonial, and the present. Use the sidebar to move through time.

Era I

Pre-Colonial Literature

Before 1565 — the age of oral tradition

Long before any foreign script reached the archipelago, Filipino communities already had a rich literary life — it simply lived in the voice, not on the page. Epics, chants, riddles, and proverbs were memorized and passed from elder to child, carrying a community's history, beliefs, and values across generations. These works served not only as entertainment but as a way of educating the community, preserving cultural heritage, and strengthening shared social values — making this era the foundation of Philippine literary history.

Oral Epics (Folk Epics)

Long narrative poems, chanted or sung, recounting the deeds of legendary heroes and the origins of a people. Performed during rituals, harvests, or communal gatherings.

Bugtong & Salawikain

Riddles (bugtong) and proverbs (salawikain) encoded practical wisdom and wit into short, memorable verse — a form of everyday teaching.

Myths & Legends

Stories explaining the origin of the world, natural phenomena, and the anito (spirits), reflecting an animist worldview shared across many ethnic groups.

Baybayin

An indigenous syllabic script used before colonization, mostly for short inscriptions and personal writing rather than long literary works — literature here still lived mainly in speech.

Characteristics of Pre-Colonial Literature

  • Oral TraditionLiterary works were passed down by word of mouth through storytelling, chanting, and singing.
  • Community-BasedStories were shared during gatherings, rituals, festivals, and other community events.
  • Rich in FolkloreIncluded epics, myths, legends, folktales, riddles (bugtong), proverbs (salawikain), and folk songs.
  • Reflects Beliefs & TraditionsLiterature expressed the customs, values, religious beliefs, and daily lives of early Filipinos.
  • Anonymous AuthorshipMost works had no known authors because they were created and preserved collectively by communities.
  • Use of Native LanguagesLiterary works were composed in indigenous languages and dialects.
  • Moral & Educational PurposeStories taught important values such as courage, respect, honesty, unity, and wisdom.
  • Connected to Nature & SpiritualityMany works featured natural elements, ancestral spirits, gods, and supernatural beings, reflecting the worldview of pre-colonial Filipinos.

Notable Epics

  • Biag ni Lam-angIlocano epic of a hero's extraordinary birth, adventures, and revenge — first transcribed by the blind Ilocano bard Pedro Bukaneg.
  • HinilawodEpic of the Sulodnon people of Panay, following the adventures of three demigod brothers; documented in the field by anthropologist F. Landa Jocano.
  • Hudhud & AlimChanted narrative poetry of the Ifugao, recited by mumbaki (ritual chanters) during harvest and ritual occasions.
  • DarangenEpic cycle of the Maranao people of Lake Lanao, telling of the hero Bantugan; studied and preserved by folklorist Mamitua Saber.
  • UlahinganEpic tradition of the Manobo people of Mindanao, recorded and translated by scholar Elena Maquiso.
Oral tradition Community & kinship Nature & the anito Heroism

Era II

Colonial Literature

1565 – 1898 — Spanish colonial period

Spanish colonization brought the printing press, the Catholic faith, and new literary forms — but for most of this period, literature was tightly bound to religion and used to teach doctrine. Only near the end of the era did Filipino writers turn literature into a tool for reform and, eventually, revolution.

Religious & Didactic Works

Doctrina Christiana (1593), the first book printed in the Philippines, taught Catholic doctrine in Tagalog and Spanish, setting the tone for early printed literature.

Pasyon

A long chanted narrative poem on the life, passion, and death of Jesus Christ, still performed by communities during Holy Week.

Awit & Korido

Metrical romances in verse, often about knights and princesses, blending European chivalric tales with local sensibilities.

Propaganda Literature

Late in the era, writers used essays, novels, and newspapers abroad to expose colonial abuses and call for reform — literature became political.

Notable Works & Writers

  • Florante at LauraFrancisco Balagtas (1838) — an awit widely considered the height of Tagalog verse.
  • Ibong AdarnaAnonymous korido about a magical bird and three princes' quest.
  • Urbana at FelizaFr. Modesto de Castro (1864) — a didactic novel in letters teaching manners and morals to Filipino youth.
  • Noli Me Tangere & El FilibusterismoJosé Rizal (1887 / 1891) — novels exposing colonial injustice, central to the Propaganda Movement.
  • La SolidaridadReformist newspaper featuring Graciano López Jaena and Marcelo H. del Pilar, published from Spain.
Faith & doctrine Chivalric romance Reform & awakening nationalism

Era III

Post-Colonial Literature

1898 – 1960s — American Occupation, Commonwealth, and early Independence

After Spain came the United States, and with it, English-language education and a free press. Filipino writers now had a new language to master and a new question to answer: what does it mean to write as a Filipino, in someone else's tongue, in a nation still finding its footing?

Literature in English

Public education introduced English widely, and a generation of Filipino writers began producing poetry, short stories, and essays in the language for the first time.

Balagtasan

A poetic debate in verse, named after Francisco Balagtas, continuing the Tagalog oral tradition alongside the new English-language writing.

Occupation-Era Writing

During the Japanese Occupation (1941–45), open publishing was restricted, pushing some literary expression underground or into allegory.

Diaspora Voices

Filipino writers abroad began documenting migrant labor and identity, a theme that would only grow in later decades.

Notable Writers

  • José García VillaPoet known for experimental style and his "comma poems"; among the first Filipino writers recognized internationally.
  • Nick JoaquínFiction and essays exploring Filipino identity across Spanish and American influence.
  • Carlos BulosanAmerica Is in the Heart — a memoir-novel of Filipino migrant labor in the United States.
  • Amado V. HernándezPoet, novelist, and labor leader; Mga Ibong Mandaragit critiques social injustice in the post-independence years.
  • N.V.M. Gonzalez & Bienvenido SantosFiction writers portraying rural life and the overseas Filipino experience.
Identity in a new language Nationhood Migration

Era IV

21st Century — Contemporary / Present

2000s – today

Today's Filipino literature is everywhere at once — in bookstores, in webnovels, in spoken-word videos, and in captions typed in Taglish. The page has not disappeared; it has multiplied, and Filipino writers are using every version of it.

Digital & Self-Publishing

Platforms for online serialized fiction and social media have let writers publish directly to readers, bypassing traditional gatekeepers.

Spoken Word & Performance

Live poetry communities have brought literature back to its oral roots, this time performed on stage and shared online.

Komiks & Graphic Novels

A revival of Filipino comics and graphic storytelling, blending visual art with narrative tradition.

Wider, Bolder Themes

Contemporary writers take on overseas labor, LGBTQ+ identity, martial law memory, urban life, and climate — often in Taglish, mixing Filipino and English freely.

Notable Contemporary Voices

  • Bob OngPseudonymous author known for humorous, conversational Filipino-language prose.
  • Ricky LeeProlific screenwriter and novelist, influential in both film and contemporary fiction.
  • Lualhati BautistaNovelist known for Dekada '70 and Gapô, tackling family, martial law, and gender with unflinching realism.
  • Online & independent poetsA growing wave of self-published poets and spoken-word artists reaching audiences directly online.
Diaspora & OFW life Identity & belonging Taglish & new forms

Contemporary · Example 01

A Little Story About My Everyday Life

A Personal Blog

Have you ever had one of those days when everything feels tiring, but somehow you still end up laughing with your friends? I experience days like that a lot as a student.

My usual day starts with getting ready for school. Sometimes, I feel sleepy and do not feel like getting up, especially when I know that I have a lot of things to do. But once I get to school and see my friends, my mood usually gets better.

Being a student is not always easy. There are assignments, activities, deadlines, and lessons that sometimes make me feel stressed. There are also times when I feel pressured because I want to do my best. Even when I get tired, I remind myself that these things are part of my journey as a student.

One thing that makes my school days better is spending time with my friends. We talk about random things, laugh at simple jokes, and share stories about what happened during our day. Sometimes, we do not even have to do anything special. Just being together can already make a tiring day more enjoyable.

As I experience these things, I have realized that life is not always about big achievements or exciting moments. Sometimes, the small things matter too. A simple conversation, a funny moment, or even a normal day can become a memory that I will remember someday.

I also learned that it is okay to have bad days. There are times when I make mistakes or things do not happen the way I want them to. Instead of being too hard on myself, I try to learn from what happened and move forward.

For me, blogging is a good way to share these thoughts. It feels like writing in a journal, but instead of keeping everything to myself, I can share my experiences and perspective with other people. I can write about my everyday life, the things I enjoy, the challenges I face, and the lessons I learn along the way.

I may still have a lot to learn, but I know that every experience is helping me grow. I do not know exactly what my future will look like, but I want to appreciate the present and enjoy the little moments while I can.

Maybe someday, when I read this again, I will realize how much I have changed. For now, I will continue making memories, learning from my experiences, and writing down the little stories that make my life meaningful.
