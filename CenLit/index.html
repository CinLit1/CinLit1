Era I

Pre-Colonial Literature

Before 1565 — the age of oral tradition

Long before any foreign script reached the archipelago, Filipino communities already had a rich literary life — it simply lived in the voice, not on the page. Epics, chants, riddles, and proverbs were memorized and passed from elder to child, carrying a community's history, beliefs, and values across generations.

Oral Epics (Folk Epics)

Long narrative poems, chanted or sung, recounting the deeds of legendary heroes and the origins of a people.

Bugtong & Salawikain

Riddles (bugtong) and proverbs (salawikain) encoded practical wisdom and wit into short, memorable verse.

Myths & Legends

Stories explaining the origin of the world, natural phenomena, and the anito (spirits), reflecting an animist worldview.

Baybayin

An indigenous syllabic script used before colonization, mostly for short inscriptions and personal writing.

Characteristics of Pre-Colonial Literature

  • Oral TraditionLiterary works were passed down by word of mouth through storytelling, chanting, and singing.
  • Community-BasedStories were shared during gatherings, rituals, festivals, and other community events.
  • Rich in FolkloreIncluded epics, myths, legends, folktales, riddles, proverbs, and folk songs.
  • Reflects Beliefs & TraditionsLiterature expressed the customs, values, religious beliefs, and daily lives of early Filipinos.
  • Anonymous AuthorshipMost works had no known authors because they were created and preserved collectively by communities.
  • Use of Native LanguagesLiterary works were composed in indigenous languages and dialects.
  • Moral & Educational PurposeStories taught important values such as courage, respect, honesty, unity, and wisdom.
  • Connected to Nature & SpiritualityMany works featured natural elements, ancestral spirits, gods, and supernatural beings.

Notable Epics

  • Biag ni Lam-angIlocano epic of a hero's extraordinary birth, adventures, and revenge — first transcribed by Pedro Bukaneg.
  • HinilawodEpic of the Sulodnon people of Panay, following the adventures of three demigod brothers.
  • Hudhud & AlimChanted narrative poetry of the Ifugao, recited by mumbaki during harvest and ritual occasions.
  • DarangenEpic cycle of the Maranao people of Lake Lanao, telling of the hero Bantugan.
  • UlahinganEpic tradition of the Manobo people of Mindanao, recorded and translated by Elena Maquiso.
Oral traditionCommunity & kinshipNature & the anitoHeroism

Era II

Colonial Literature

1565 – 1898 — Spanish colonial period

Philippine literature under foreign rule did not move through one colonial period, but three — Spanish, American, and Japanese — each imposing a different language, censorship regime, and set of literary forms, and each answered in turn by Filipino writers.

1. Spanish Colonial Period

1565 – 1898

The Spanish Colonial Period lasted for more than 300 years. During this time, Spanish influence greatly affected Filipino culture, education, religion, and literature. Early literature was mostly connected to Christianity because Spanish missionaries used written works to spread Catholic teachings.

Religious & Didactic Works

Doctrina Christiana (1593), the first book printed in the Philippines, taught Catholic doctrine in Tagalog and Spanish.

Pasyon

A long chanted narrative poem on the life, passion, and death of Jesus Christ.

Awit & Korido

Metrical romances in verse, often about knights and princesses, blending European chivalric tales with local sensibilities.

Propaganda Literature

Late in the era, writers used essays, novels, and newspapers abroad to expose colonial abuses and call for reform.

Main Characteristics of Spanish Colonial Literature

  • Strong influence of Christianity and Catholicism
  • Use of Spanish and native Philippine languages
  • Religious and moral themes
  • Later works focused on nationalism and social problems
  • Literature became a way of criticizing colonial abuses
  • Development of Filipino national consciousness

Notable Works & Writers

  • Florante at LauraFrancisco Balagtas (1838) — an awit widely considered the height of Tagalog verse.
  • Ibong AdarnaAnonymous korido about a magical bird and three princes' quest.
  • Urbana at FelizaFr. Modesto de Castro (1864) — a didactic novel in letters teaching manners and morals to Filipino youth.
  • Noli Me TangereJosé Rizal (1887) — exposed abuse of power, discrimination, corruption, and friar influence under colonial rule.
  • El FilibusterismoJosé Rizal (1891) — the darker sequel to Noli Me Tangere, centered on injustice, revenge, and the desire for change.
  • La SolidaridadReformist newspaper featuring Graciano López Jaena and Marcelo H. del Pilar.
Faith & doctrineChivalric romanceReform & awakening nationalism

2. American Colonial Period

1898 – 1946

After Spanish rule ended, the Philippines came under American control. This brought major changes to Philippine literature. One of the biggest changes was the introduction of the English language through the American education system.

Main Characteristics of American Colonial Literature

  • English became an important literary language
  • Short stories, novels, poetry, and essays became popular
  • American literary styles influenced Filipino writers
  • Writers explored love, society, identity, and everyday life
  • Filipino writers began developing their own style in English
  • Modern literary forms became more common

Notable Works

  • A Child of SorrowZoilo Galang (1921) — recognized as the first Filipino novel written in English.
  • Dead StarsPaz Márquez-Benítez (1925) — an early and influential Filipino short story in English.
Literature in EnglishIdentity & societyModern literary forms

3. Japanese Colonial Period / Occupation

1941 – 1945

The Japanese Period began when Japan invaded and occupied the Philippines during World War II. The occupation greatly affected Filipino writers and the development of literature. Literature written in English was heavily restricted, and many English-language publications were stopped or censored.

Main Characteristics of Japanese Colonial Literature

  • English literature was heavily restricted and censored
  • Filipino and other local languages became more important
  • Short stories and poetry became popular
  • Haiku and tanaga were used
  • Common themes included nationalism, suffering, love of country, and life during wartime
  • Literature became a way for Filipinos to express their experiences despite restrictions

Notable Works

  • Lupang TinubuanNarciso G. Reyes — a winning short story from Ang Pinakamabuting Maikling Kathang Pilipino ng 1943.
  • Uhaw ang Tigang na LupaLiwayway Arceo — a short story reflecting the hardships and emotional struggles of Filipinos during wartime.

Era III

21st Century — Contemporary / Present

2000s – today

Today's Filipino literature is everywhere at once — in bookstores, in webnovels, in spoken-word videos, and in captions typed in Taglish. The page has not disappeared; it has multiplied, and Filipino writers are using every version of it.

Digital & Self-Publishing

Platforms for online serialized fiction and social media have let writers publish directly to readers, bypassing traditional gatekeepers.

Spoken Word & Performance

Live poetry communities have brought literature back to its oral roots, this time performed on stage and shared online.

Komiks & Graphic Novels

A revival of Filipino comics and graphic storytelling, blending visual art with narrative tradition.

Wider, Bolder Themes

Contemporary writers take on overseas labor, LGBTQ+ identity, martial law memory, urban life, and climate — often in Taglish.

Notable Contemporary Voices

  • Bob OngPseudonymous author known for humorous, conversational Filipino-language prose.
  • Ricky LeeProlific screenwriter and novelist, influential in both film and contemporary fiction.
  • Lualhati BautistaNovelist known for Dekada '70 and Gapô, tackling family, martial law, and gender with unflinching realism.
  • Online & independent poetsA growing wave of self-published poets and spoken-word artists reaching audiences directly online.
Diaspora & OFW lifeIdentity & belongingTaglish & new forms

Canonical Works

Canonical Literature

The foundational texts of Philippine literary tradition

These are the works that have shaped Philippine literature — the novels, epics, and collections that are widely studied, referenced, and celebrated as part of the national literary canon. They represent the voices and visions that have defined Filipino identity across generations.

What is Canonical Literature?

Canonical works are those texts that have achieved a privileged status in literary culture — they are taught in schools, cited in scholarship, and recognized as essential to understanding a national literary tradition. In the Philippines, the canon includes works from the colonial period to the present, in multiple languages.

Why It Matters

The canon provides a shared cultural reference point, shaping how Filipinos understand their literary history and national identity. While the canon is always evolving, these core texts remain foundational to Philippine literary education and criticism.

Six Canonical Works

Noli Me Tangere

José Rizal · 1887

A novel that exposed the abuses of Spanish colonial rule and the friars, becoming the spark for the Philippine Revolution.

El Filibusterismo

José Rizal · 1891

The darker sequel to Noli, exploring themes of revenge and revolution through the character of Simoun.

Florante at Laura

Francisco Balagtas · 1838

The masterwork of Tagalog poetry, an awit that uses a chivalric romance to critique Spanish colonial society.

Biag ni Lam-ang

Ilocano Epic · pre-colonial

The Ilocano epic, one of the oldest surviving Philippine epics, preserved through oral tradition and later transcribed.

Dead Stars

Paz Márquez-Benítez · 1925

A landmark short story in English that helped establish the modern Filipino short story tradition.

Dekada '70

Lualhati Bautista · 1983

A novel that chronicles the lives of a Filipino family during the Martial Law years under Ferdinand Marcos.

Regional Literary Treasures

The Philippines is an archipelago of diverse cultures and languages. Here are two canonical works from each major island group — Luzon, Visayas, and Mindanao — that have shaped regional and national literary traditions.

LUZON Ilocos, Tagalog, Bicol, and more

Biag ni Lam-ang

Ilocano Epic · Pre-colonial

The epic hero Lam-ang is born with the ability to speak, embarks on a journey to avenge his father's death, and performs extraordinary feats — including bringing himself back to life with the help of his magical pets.

Significance: One of the few complete pre-colonial epics preserved through oral tradition and transcribed by Pedro Bukaneg, showcasing Ilocano values of courage, loyalty, and family honor.

Florante at Laura

Francisco Balagtas · 1838

An awit (metrical romance) written in Tagalog that tells the story of Duke Florante of Albania and Princess Laura of Crotona, set against a backdrop of war, betrayal, and political intrigue.

Significance: While set in a European fantasy kingdom, the poem is a thinly veiled critique of Spanish colonial rule and remains the most celebrated work in Tagalog literature.

VISAYAS Cebuano, Hiligaynon, Waray, and more

Hinilawod

Sulodnon Epic · Pre-colonial (Panay)

One of the longest surviving epics in the world, with over 28,000 verses. It tells the story of the demigod brothers Labaw Donggon, Humadapnon, and Dumalapdap, who embark on epic quests filled with adventure, love, and supernatural beings.

Significance: Preserves the culture, beliefs, and values of the Sulodnon people of Panay, offering a window into pre-colonial Visayan society.

Ang Pasyon ni Jesu Kristo

Various Authors · Spanish Colonial

The Pasyon is a narrative poem about the life, passion, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ, widely recited during Holy Week. The most famous version is the "Pasyon Pilapil" published in 1814.

Significance: Though religious in nature, the Pasyon became a vehicle for Filipino expression and resistance, with different regional versions emerging in Cebuano, Hiligaynon, and other Visayan languages.

MINDANAO Maranao, Manobo, Tausug, and more

Darangen

Maranao Epic · Pre-colonial (Lanao)

An epic cycle of the Maranao people, consisting of 17 chapters. It tells the story of the hero Bantugan and his adventures, including his journey to the land of the dead and his eventual return.

Significance: Declared a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage, the Darangen preserves the history, customs, and values of the Maranao people and is still chanted by traditional storytellers today.

Ulahingan

Manobo Epic · Pre-colonial (Bukidnon)

An epic tradition of the Manobo people of Mindanao, chronicling the adventures of the hero Agyu and his people as they journey to find a promised land called "Nalandangan."

Significance: Recorded and translated by Elena Maquiso, the Ulahingan is a testament to the rich oral tradition of the Manobo and their worldview, reflecting their relationship with nature, spirits, and community.

A Tapestry of Voices

These regional works represent just a fraction of the Philippines' rich literary heritage. From the epic chants of the Cordilleras to the folk songs of the Visayas and the Islamic traditions of Mindanao, Philippine literature is a mosaic of languages, cultures, and histories — each region contributing its unique voice to the national narrative.

Contemporary · Example 01

A Little Story About My Everyday Life

A Personal Blog

Have you ever had one of those days when everything feels tiring, but somehow you still end up laughing with your friends? I experience days like that a lot as a student.

My usual day starts with getting ready for school. Sometimes, I feel sleepy and do not feel like getting up, especially when I know that I have a lot of things to do. But once I get to school and see my friends, my mood usually gets better.

Being a student is not always easy. There are assignments, activities, deadlines, and lessons that sometimes make me feel stressed. There are also times when I feel pressured because I want to do my best. Even when I get tired, I remind myself that these things are part of my journey as a student.

One thing that makes my school days better is spending time with my friends. We talk about random things, laugh at simple jokes, and share stories about what happened during our day. Sometimes, we do not even have to do anything special. Just being together can already make a tiring day more enjoyable.

As I experience these things, I have realized that life is not always about big achievements or exciting moments. Sometimes, the small things matter too. A simple conversation, a funny moment, or even a normal day can become a memory that I will remember someday.

I also learned that it is okay to have bad days. There are times when I make mistakes or things do not happen the way I want them to. Instead of being too hard on myself, I try to learn from what happened and move forward.

For me, blogging is a good way to share these thoughts. It feels like writing in a journal, but instead of keeping everything to myself, I can share my experiences and perspective with other people. I can write about my everyday life, the things I enjoy, the challenges I face, and the lessons I learn along the way.

I may still have a lot to learn, but I know that every experience is helping me grow. I do not know exactly what my future will look like, but I want to appreciate the present and enjoy the little moments while I can.

Maybe someday, when I read this again, I will realize how much I have changed. For now, I will continue making memories, learning from my experiences, and writing down the little stories that make my life meaningful.

Contemporary · Example 04

How to Be Mia Reyes

Chick Lit · Contemporary coming-of-age

Anak, how do you put in your information?
Mia, anak, can you buy food on your way home?
Mia, please help your younger sister with her project. You're the older sibling, after all.
Mia, when you grow up, I want you to become a doctor, ha?
Mia, you should smile more!
You're very ambitious. You should become a lawyer!
Don't marry too early, Mia.
Just marry rich!

These were sentences I had heard so many times that they had practically become background music.

As Mia Reyes, people expected great things from me. They expected me to have a bright future. They expected me to become so many things—a doctor, a lawyer, a teacher, maybe even an astronaut if they were feeling particularly creative that day.

The only problem was that nobody could agree on what that future was supposed to be.

I'm Mia Reyes, and I'm everyone's version of who I should be. Yet I'm also Mia Reyes, a seventeen-year-old girl who keeps a notebook filled with other people's opinions about her.

It started as a joke. At least, that's what I told myself. The first page simply read: Mia should become a doctor. The next page said: Mia should become a lawyer. By page twenty-three, I was beginning to suspect that nobody actually knew what they were talking about.

I write them every day as if my life depends on it—maybe it does. Might be. I don't know. I'm on page thirty-six now, and somehow, it doesn't get any better.

It's hilarious, really. To keep track of things, I have a personal notebook called How to Be Mia Reyes. Written by everyone except Mia Reyes.

"Mia!" Mom shouts from the living room. I already know what she needs. Based on page four: Be available anytime for your family. Personal Note: Like a customer service hotline, except unpaid.

I close my notebook and sigh. "Coming!"

The moment I step into the living room, Mom hands me her phone. "Anak, why is Facebook asking me for my password again?" I stare at the screen. "Because you logged out, Ma." "Oh." A pause. "Can you fix it?" Of course I can. Page four never lies.

A few minutes later, I return the phone and head back to my room. Before I can sit down— "Mia!" I freeze. Then slowly open my notebook. Page 17: Learn how to be in two places at once. Personal Note: Still working on it.

"Mia, can you buy food later?" Mom asks. "Okay." I make a new entry. Page 37: Always say yes. Personal Note: Saying no causes visible confusion among relatives.

I barely finish writing before my younger sister barges into my room. "Ate, help." "With what?" "My project." I look at her. I look at the glue. I look at the cartolina. I look at the glitter that somehow ended up on my desk despite never being mine. Then I slowly reach for my notebook. Page 23: Assist younger siblings with school projects. Personal Note: Assist is a funny word. What they really mean is "save."

My sister grins. "Ate, you're the best." And just like that, I find myself cutting paper flowers at eleven in the evening for a project that isn't mine.

Honestly, if someone ever steals my notebook, they'd probably think I was running a very strange business. Services offered by Mia Reyes include: Technical support, Academic support, Emotional support, Food delivery, Life planning. No refunds.

I stare at the ceiling, waiting for the day to end. I ask myself, What do I even aspire to become? Before I could even come up with an answer of my own, I fell asleep.

Contemporary · Example 06

The Last Bus Home

Flash Fiction · A very short story

The last bus from the city was half-empty. She sat by the window, watching the streetlights blur into streaks of gold.

At the next stop, a man boarded. He wore a worn-out jacket and carried a guitar case. He sat across the aisle, not looking at anyone.

She noticed his hands. They were calloused, but his fingers moved gently, as if tracing notes on an invisible fretboard.

He didn't play. He just stared out the window, the same way she did.

For a moment, she wanted to say something. Where are you going? What song are you carrying?

But the bus rumbled on, and the words stayed in her throat.

When she got off at her stop, she looked back. He was still there, eyes fixed on the dark road ahead.

She wondered if he had a story, too. If everyone on that bus was carrying something — a song, a secret, a silence — that they couldn't put down.

The doors closed. The bus pulled away. And the city swallowed another unsaid goodbye.

— Sometimes, the shortest stories leave the longest echoes.

About Flash Fiction
Flash fiction is a very short story, typically under 1,000 words. It focuses on a single moment, emotion, or image, leaving much unsaid.
